\begin{takeaway}
\textbf{One-paragraph thesis.} The four names Creo asked us to benchmark are not
four versions of the same competitor; they are four \emph{archetypes} of how an
advisory brand reaches senior buyers. \textbf{Craig Group} is a PE-exclusive go-to-market boutique that wins on a
razor-sharp niche, trademarked products timed to the deal cycle, and founder-led
association networking. \textbf{Rick Williams} is a one-person thought-leadership
brand that wins on a single trademarked idea, an email list he claims at 50,000
readers, and relentless repurposing of each piece of writing across newsletter,
bylines, podcast, and keynotes. \textbf{Quadrillion Partners} is an operator-led
boutique that wins on quotable anti-consultant positioning, a catalog of eight
named, priced, outcome-ranged sprints, and a coordinated book-launch content stack.
\textbf{Bridgetown Research} is a venture-backed AI platform that sells
``AI business analysts'' to PE funds and consultancies and now runs its own AI-native
strategy arm (Polaris); it wins on blue-chip logos, hard speed claims, and an
enterprise-grade trust story, and it is the one benchmark that competes with Creo's
\emph{delivery model} rather than its marketing. None of the four has what Creo's brief asks for --- a sustained engine that puts a
message in front of 2,000+ decision makers every 8--12 weeks --- and each has a
visible gap Creo can occupy. The practical conclusion: \textbf{copy the mechanics
(named offers, email-first capture, founder-as-channel, sequenced launches), avoid
the failure modes (bursty cadence, unaudited reach claims, hero-dependence,
inconsistent numbers), and differentiate on the three things none of them can show:
a named team, attributed proof, and a cadence that actually holds.}
\end{takeaway}

\section{Purpose, Scope, and an Identification Note}
% ======================================================================

\subsection{Why these four}
Creo's project brief asks the team to sharpen the firm's value proposition, segment its
buyers, and build a ``scalable, repeatable digital marketing engine'' with a message in
front of 2,000+ decision makers every 8--12 weeks. The client then named four
benchmarks. We read that list as a request to answer four questions for each:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{What do they sell, to whom, and how is it packaged?} (positioning,
  productization, pricing signals)
  \item \textbf{What proof do they show?} (and how much of it is verifiable)
  \item \textbf{How does their marketing engine actually run?} (channel by channel,
  with cadence and audience-size evidence)
  \item \textbf{What should Creo copy, avoid, or position against?}
\end{enumerate}

Everything here comes from public sources read on 19--20 October 2026, and on 28
October 2026 for Bridgetown Research (firm websites,
LinkedIn, press wires, podcast directories, publisher pages, company registries).
Figures are tagged \self{} where only the firm asserts them. Items we could not confirm
are listed in \S\ref{sec:unverified} rather than silently dropped.

\subsection{A note on Bridgetown Research}
Bridgetown is not a boutique and does not market like one. It is a software company
(\$19M Series A from Accel and Lightspeed, February 2025) whose product does part of
what a value-creation advisor bills for --- expert interviews, market sizing,
commercial diligence --- in a day instead of weeks, and which in 2026 began selling
that capability as a service through its own AI-native strategy firm, Polaris. We
benchmark its marketing on the same four questions as the others, but its real
significance for Creo is strategic: it is both the clearest substitution threat in the
set and a possible delivery partner (\S\ref{sec:bridgetown}).

\section{Craig Group}
% ======================================================================

\subsection{Snapshot \high}
Craig Group is a Houston-based, technology-enabled sales and marketing advisory firm
that works ``exclusively with private-equity-backed, lower middle-market companies.''
Founder Summer Craig came out of HomeAway (through its IPO), Omnicom, Publicis, AT\&T,
and AOL, and ran a large marketing budget at Gulf States Toyota, the firm's first
client. The leadership page lists nine leaders (including partners hired from SBI's PE
practice and Consero in 2024--26), about 25 ``Go-To-Market Principals'' (a bench of
fractional advisors), and roughly 11 delivery staff spanning RevOps, paid search,
copy, design, and video. Industries: industrial, manufacturing, energy, SaaS,
healthcare, B2B services. The firm is a HubSpot Gold and Google partner, an ACG
Houston Silver annual sponsor, and Summer Craig joined the ACG Houston board in
September 2025.

\textbf{Business-model signals} \self{} (from the founder's own podcast interviews):
a typical new engagement is about \$150K; each partner is expected to land one new
client per quarter; gross margin near 70\%; customer-acquisition cost near 30\% of
revenue (which she says she wants to halve); sales cycles of nine months or more
because sponsors vet the firm first and then introduce it to portfolio CEOs; and the
firm has ``engaged about 300 of about 3,000'' target PE firms. Early growth was
roughly 90\% referral.

\subsection{Positioning and messaging \high}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Hero: \emph{``Built for Private Equity. Powered by Results.''} /
  \emph{``Accelerating Revenue Growth for PE-Backed Businesses''} /
  \emph{``From Growth Stalls To Scalable Growth Strategy.''}
  \item Organizing idea: \emph{``Revenue is a Team Sport''} (marketing, sales, and
  customer success aligned); \emph{``Impact that Adds Total Enterprise Value''};
  \emph{``a tech-savvy extension of your management team''}; \emph{``in months, not
  years.''}
  \item The founder states publicly that she \textbf{dropped the phrase ``strategic
  digital advisory''} because PE investors did not understand it, and re-languaged
  everything around value creation, enterprise value, and exit multiples. This is the
  single most transferable messaging lesson in the benchmark set.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Services and productization \high}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Trademarked system.} The CG\texttrademark{} Process (Discover / Launch /
  Evaluate) maps to three named products: \textbf{Catalyst\texttrademark{}} (diagnostic
  and GTM plan), \textbf{Ignite\texttrademark{}} (ongoing execution and RevOps), and
  \textbf{Conduit\texttrademark{}} (analytics; since January 2025 a machine-learning
  forecasting tool, extended in November 2025 to ``board-ready scenario planning, on
  demand'').
  \item \textbf{Deal-cycle-timed offers.} \emph{GTM Due Diligence} (a ``rapid 2--3 week
  sprint'' producing red, yellow, or green risk flags and a 100-day plan); \emph{GTM
  Exit-Readiness Assessment} (6--8 weeks, run 12--18 months before exit, feeding the
  CIM and equity story); Strategic Growth Alignment; Sales Improvement Training.
  \item \textbf{Delivery model.} Strategy plus embedded execution --- the firm builds
  the CRM/martech stack, runs campaigns, and provides fractional or interim CRO
  capacity. Pricing is described as ``fully transparent'' with ``options for
  performance-based structure''; no rate card is published.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Proof and claims}
Self-reported case studies include ``doubled revenue'' at a named client, a PE-backed
equipment-rental company whose leads went from 30 to 120 per month, win rate from 10\%
to 65\%, and job margin from 25\% to 43\%, and a manufacturer with ``ROI up to 20x.'' A
sponsored-style CEOWORLD piece (May 2024) calls the firm a ``trusted partner to more
than a dozen PE firms'' and names AEA Investors, Cyprium, Energize Capital, and
Golden Gate Capital. Third-party footprint is limited to ACG sponsor and board
listings, a Kayo Conference Series community spotlight (June 2026), and wire-release
syndication. \textbf{No sponsor appears on a case study, and no metric is
independently verified.} Awards are marketing-craft awards (AMA Houston, BMA Houston)
rather than PE or consulting recognition.

\subsection{Strengths and weaknesses}
\textbf{Strengths.} (1) A one-line exclusion (``exclusively PE-backed LMM'') that
makes the firm instantly legible to sponsors. (2) Products named, trademarked, and
timed to the deal cycle (diligence $\rightarrow$ 100-day $\rightarrow$ exit-readiness)
with fixed durations and outputs a deal team can drop into an IC memo. (3)
Investor-language discipline. (4) Deep ACG/Kayo embedding plus a repeatable PR habit.
(5) The founder as the visible face: podcast guesting, panel moderation, a board seat.

\textbf{Weaknesses.} (1) Small reach and a bursty cadence --- quiet for most of 2025,
a sprint in early 2026 --- nothing like an every-8--12-weeks engine. (2) The newsletter
is a bare box with no name, archive, or cadence; no owned podcast, no annual survey or
benchmark, no gated asset. (3) Proof is anonymized and self-reported. (4) A
Texas/ACG-centric footprint with little Northeast visibility. (5) Scope is sales,
marketing, and RevOps only; no pricing, operations, integration, or P\&L-wide value
creation. Growth remains mostly referral, with CAC the founder herself calls too high.

\section{Rick Williams / \emph{Create the Future}}
% ======================================================================

\subsection{Snapshot \high}
Rick Williams is a Boston-area solo thought-leadership brand built on one trademarked
concept (Create the Future\textregistered{}), one book (\emph{Create the Future: Powerful
Decision-Making Tools for Your Company and Yourself}, Amplify Publishing, September
2024), one five-step framework (Define the challenge / Imagine success / Create options
/ Evaluate barriers / Choose the future), and a hub-and-spoke content engine. His
credibility stack --- physicist, Harvard Business School, Arthur D. Little, founder and
CEO of a multifamily real-estate company, board chair --- is repeated verbatim on every
surface. The legal entity, Williams Advisory Partners, LLC, offers ``CEO and board
advisory services for middle market technology companies'' but has no clients,
pricing, case studies, or independent press footprint. \textbf{His audience is general
CEOs, founders, and directors of \$5--300M private companies, not PE sponsors.}

\subsection{Positioning and messaging \high}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{``Helping Leaders Succeed.''} / \emph{``Leaders create the future by the
  decisions they make.''} / \emph{``Create the Future is about how to decide --- not what
  to decide.''}
  \item \emph{``An engaged board of directors is a value accelerator for every
  company''}; the CEO of a \$5--300M company has a ``lonely job.''
  \item The framework is pitched as replacing ``a \$500,000 consulting engagement'' ---
  i.e., he positions \emph{against} firms like Creo, selling tools instead of hours.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What he sells}
Board seats (several life-science and fintech directorships, with CEO coaching bundled
in); CEO/board advisory under Williams Advisory Partners; two keynotes (``Making
Difficult Decisions in a Time of Change'' and ``Make Your Board of Directors into a
Value Accelerator'') with a key-takeaways handout; a Create the Future\textregistered{}
workshop for leaders ``and their coaches and consultants''; and the book (\$35 retail,
\$5 off on his site, Kindle and Audible, a free introductory chapter as the lead
magnet, and a bulk-quote option). No fees are published and no speaker-bureau listing
was found.

A notable mechanic: his podcast \textbf{guest-application form} screens applicants by

\subsection{Strengths and weaknesses}
\textbf{Strengths.} (1) Ruthless message discipline --- one owned, trademarked concept
and one phrase set, repeated everywhere. (2) Email-first: capture on every page; the
list is the asset, and each piece is written once for the newsletter and then
syndicated to blog, CEOWORLD, Wiley, and podcast topics. (3) Pre-packaged, dated sales
collateral (speaker sheet, keynote summaries, media kit, free chapter) that lowers
friction for bookers. (4) A borrowed-audience loop and relentless citation of
high-profile endorsers. (5) Newsjacking (executive pay, board firings) keeps governance
content timely.

\textbf{Weaknesses.} (1) A personal brand, not a firm engine: no team, no case
studies, no named clients, no service pages. (2) Audience mismatch for Creo --- no PE
sponsors, operating partners, or portfolio CEOs anywhere in guests, venues, or
articles. (3) Reach claims are unaudited and public engagement signals are thin (one
Apple rating; three Goodreads ratings). (4) Social channels are neglected, so the
newsletter does its work in private with no visible social-proof flywheel. (5)
Content is reflective and essayistic rather than outcome-quantified --- little a PE
diligence lens would score as evidence.

\section{Quadrillion Partners}
% ======================================================================

\subsection{Snapshot \high}
Quadrillion Partners (legal entity Quadrillion Technology Partners, LLC; founded 2012;
Plano, Texas) describes itself as ``operator-led performance improvement for PE-backed
and public companies'' delivering ``EBITDA, margin, and working capital gains through
execution sprints.'' The only person named anywhere on the site is founder
\textbf{George Stelling} (first CIO of NVIDIA; pricing and transformation at ON
Semiconductor under TPG; interim FP\&A/M\&A at Synaptics; Bain and PwC; co-founder of
Prophet Equity, 2007--11; Tuck MBA). Directories list about six employees; the team
page returns a 404; the ``Network'' page promises ``zero consultants'' on
success-based compensation but names no members. Stelling also runs three affiliated
brands --- Lunation (an execution-tracking platform), Brilliant Build (digital-twin
simulation), and Silicon Century Capital (an investing vehicle with an empty site).
In October 2026 he published \emph{Decipher \& Deliver}, whose title doubles as the
firm's tagline.

\subsection{Positioning and messaging \high}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{``Decipher \& Deliver.''} / \emph{``Most plans fail in execution, not
  strategy. We run both.''} / \emph{``Outputs are captured value, not decks.''}
  \item \emph{``Consultancies recommend. QP delivers.''} --- explicit anti-positioning
  against McKinsey, Bain, and BCG, against PE operating partners (``surge capacity''),
  and against interim-staffing firms (``a staffing firm owns the placement, QP owns the
  result'').
  \item PE page: \emph{``Hit the bridge, protect the return.''} / \emph{``90-to-180-day
  sprints tied to the bridge, not 18-month transformations.''} Public-company page:
  \emph{``The quarter is the unit of trust.''}
  \item LinkedIn headline: \emph{``EBITDA, cash \& enterprise value in 90 days.''}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Services and productization \high}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Three-phase model:} Plan (co-author the value-creation plan in
  diligence, first 100 days, or mid-hold; ``fixed fee, portable'') $\rightarrow$ Execute
  (8--12-week sprints with a senior lead and a weekly CEO/CFO cadence) $\rightarrow$
  Realize (measured in EBITDA, margin, working capital, revenue).
  \item \textbf{Eight named sprints}, each with a trigger condition, duration, and
  outcome range: pricing and margin (8 wks, 200--400 bps), commercial operations (10
  wks, 5--15\% revenue), digital and AI (12 wks, 15--25\% IT cost), operational
  throughput (10 wks, 15--30\%), procurement (12 wks, 8--12\%), working capital (8 wks,
  10--20\%), SG\&A (10 wks, 10--20\%), finance transformation (8 wks, 30\%+ faster
  close). Interim CTrO/CFO/COO/CIO seats ``with decision rights and P\&L accountability.''
  \item \textbf{Published commercial model:} ``Fixed fee per sprint, plus an optional
  success fee tied to defined KPIs. No hourly billing.'' Fit is \$200M--\$3B revenue;
  the FAQ explicitly declines sub-\$50M, pre-revenue, and ``diligence-only / pure
  advisory'' work --- a public \emph{not-a-fit} list, which is rare for a boutique.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Proof and claims}
Self-reported: ``\$1.4B+ enterprise value since 2012'' on LinkedIn and the book site
(the FAQ says \$1.2B+); \$100M of EBITDA captured in nine months as interim Chief
Transformation Officer at ConvergeOne (the only named client); eight anonymized case
studies with dollar figures (e.g., ``\$77M gross-margin uplift, \$400M document-solutions
provider''); ten homepage testimonials attributed only by descriptor (``\$5B Private
Equity Sponsor''). Third-party: one paid PR Newswire release (6 Oct 2026), three book
endorsements, 16 LinkedIn recommendations. \textbf{No awards, press coverage, or
analyst mentions were found.} Several credibility leaks are visible to any careful
buyer: the two different enterprise-value totals, sprint lengths stated as both 8--12
weeks and 90--180 days, a duplicated problem statement across two sprint cards, and
``Lorem ipsum'' placeholder labels on the contact page.

\subsection{Strengths and weaknesses}
\textbf{Strengths.} (1) Extremely sharp, quotable positioning built on a single
contrast (operators vs.\ consultants), with a book title that doubles as the tagline.
(2) A productized catalog: eight named sprints with duration and outcome range, a
published fee model, and a public not-a-fit list. (3) Dense quantified proof and a
credible founder CV. (4) A textbook thought-leadership \emph{launch stack} on a tiny
budget: book $\rightarrow$ microsite $\rightarrow$ wire release $\rightarrow$ podcast
$\rightarrow$ weekly derivative posts $\rightarrow$ founder LinkedIn amplification, all
inside six weeks. (5) Frictionless conversion --- a calendar link on every page.

\textbf{Weaknesses.} (1) A single-person firm in practice, with a 404 team page and a
nameless ``network.'' (2) Tiny audiences and no newsletter. (3) A content engine seven
weeks old and entirely book-driven; sustainability unproven. (4) Inconsistent numbers
and placeholder text that undercut an ``operators, not consultants'' promise. (5)
Brand sprawl across four Stelling-led entities. (6) No upper-funnel nurture --- no gated
tool, webinar, or benchmark report; the funnel jumps from a LinkedIn post straight to
``book a call.''

\section{Bridgetown Research}\label{sec:bridgetown}
% ======================================================================

\subsection{Snapshot \high}
Bridgetown Research was co-founded in December 2023 by CEO \textbf{Harsh Sahai}, a former
McKinsey consultant who ran diligences there and later led machine-learning teams at
Amazon. It raised a \textbf{\$19M Series A co-led by Accel and Lightspeed} in February
2025. At that point it reported about fourteen customers (two in the U.K., a dozen in
the U.S.): PE and VC funds, consulting firms, and corporate M\&A teams. Press coverage
places it in Seattle. LinkedIn lists San Francisco and Bangalore, 64 employees, and
4,174 followers; open roles in 2025--26 were mostly engineering and associate hires in
Bangalore and Mumbai. The website names nobody: the team is described only as ``former
consultants, investors, operators, and AI researchers.''

The firm sells three things. \textbf{Teamroom} is ``a full-stack agentic research and
analysis platform'' built around \textbf{``Bridget,''} an AI business analyst that
sources experts, runs AI voice interviews with them, builds the fact base, and drafts
the analysis. \textbf{Polaris}, launched in 2026, is ``a frontier strategy firm built
around AI from the ground up'': lean teams of practitioners delivering diligence and
strategy work on the platform. \textbf{Bridgetown Alliances} matches opt-in independent
advisors with clients. At the Series A, Bridgetown said clients could use the agents
directly or bring in ``an independent consultant or small consulting firm'' to work
with them. In other words, firms of Creo's size are a channel it designed for.

\subsection{Positioning and messaging \high}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Hero: \emph{``AI business analysts for strategic decision-making''} /
  \emph{``Research and analyses for leading advisors, investors and operators.''}
  \item Product lines: \emph{``Pairing the best of human judgement with agentic
  horsepower''}; \emph{``Proactive by default. Sharper with every project''};
  \emph{``Get better results on strategy work than with general-purpose AI tools.''}
  \item Category framing: an ``AI-Enabled vs.\ AI-Native'' blog argument that next-generation
  consulting firms will be \emph{built around} AI rather than add it on. Polaris
  presents itself as the proof of that argument.
  \item Trust framing: \emph{``Your data stays yours --- always''} and ``never used to
  train our models, or any third-party models.'' Security takes a full homepage section.
\end{itemize}
Bridgetown sells \textbf{speed and capacity, not judgment}. ``Human judgement'' is the
part it leaves to the buyer or to Polaris's practitioners, and that is where Creo's
value proposition sits.

\subsection{Services and productization \high}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Named product and persona.} Teamroom is the workspace and ``Bridget'' is
  the analyst. It works ``in Teamroom, through chat, from Claude, or by email'' and
  connects to Gmail, Claude, OpenAI, and Gemini via MCP. The company claims coverage
  of 12,000+ markets and 100+ ``expert-built skills.''
  \item \textbf{Use-case menu written in deal language:} commercial due diligence,
  pre-LOI diligence, value-creation planning, quality-of-revenue review, customer
  reference calls, synergy assessment, red-flag reporting, pricing and packaging,
  win-loss, churn, and channel strategy. Most of these are value-creation tasks, and
  several are things Creo bills for today.
  \item \textbf{Commercial model.} No prices are published. The only signal is
  ``available for enterprise engagements,'' and every CTA goes to ``Book a demo.''
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Proof and claims}
Of the four benchmarks, Bridgetown has the strongest \emph{attributed} proof. Its
``used by teams at'' row shows \textbf{SoftBank, Partners Group, Apax, Shamrock
Capital, and 26North}, and it publishes named customer stories for \textbf{Equinix}
(data-center market monitoring) and \textbf{Cloudera} (always-on go-to-market
analysis). The metrics are self-reported: ``500+'' projects, ``80,000+'' hours saved,
``10,000+'' AI-moderated expert conversations, expert-interview synthesis ``$\sim$90\%
faster,'' market research cut from ``3--5 days to same day,'' and IC-memo drafting
``$\sim$50\% faster.'' At the Series A it claimed an initial diligence report ``within
24 hours.'' Trust credentials (SOC 2 Type II, ISO 27001, GDPR, SSO/MFA, single-tenant
deployment, MNPI/PII screening of expert calls) appear on the homepage. Third-party
validation is funding coverage (TechCrunch, GeekWire, a Lightspeed investment memo)
and a Tercera 30 listing. \textbf{Logos show who uses it; none of the stories puts a
number on a client outcome}. The Teamroom page's benchmark chart against OpenAI Codex
and Claude Cowork displays every score as zero, and both Polaris case studies read
``Coming soon.''

\subsection{Strengths and weaknesses}
\textbf{Strengths.} (1) Blue-chip, named logos, which a diligence-minded buyer will take

(hours, days, percentages) that any deal team can test. (3) Security and data handling
treated as marketing, which answers the first objection a sponsor raises about AI and
confidential deal data. (4) A named product with a persona (``Bridget'') that is easy
to demo and to remember. (5) A two-sided model, platform plus service arm plus advisor
network, that grows through other consultancies rather than against them.

\textbf{Weaknesses.} (1) No named people anywhere on the site: no founder bio, no
team, no authors. In a relationship market, that reads as a vendor, not an advisor.
(2) Almost no content engine: a handful of undated posts, no newsletter, sparse

capacity, not accountability for outcomes. Nothing on the site claims EBITDA

all-zero benchmark chart, ``Coming soon'' case studies) undercut an enterprise-grade promise. (5) Polaris is a prototype with no
named engagements and no clear owner, and it competes with the consulting firms
Bridgetown also wants as customers.

\section{Implications for Creo: Copy, Avoid, Differentiate}
% ======================================================================

\subsection{Copy the mechanics}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Name and number the offers (QP, Craig).} Creo's four service lines
  should read like sprint cards: a trigger (``when the 100-day plan is slipping''), a
  fixed duration, a named owner, and an outcome range Creo can substantiate. Time them
  to the deal cycle --- diligence, first 100 days, mid-hold, 12--18 months pre-exit ---
  the way Craig Group does, so a deal team knows \emph{when} to call.
  \item \textbf{Speak investor language everywhere (Craig).} Summer Craig's public
  lesson --- that sponsors did not understand ``strategic digital advisory'' --- applies
  directly to ``peak performance'' and ``exemplary service.'' Lead with EBITDA bridge,
  enterprise value, hold period, and exit multiple.
  \item \textbf{Publish the commercial model and the not-a-fit list (QP).} A public
  statement of fee structure and minimum engagement size is a trust signal in a
  credence-good market and pre-qualifies inbound.
  \item \textbf{Make the founder the channel (QP, Craig).} Company pages do not carry
  boutiques; both PE-facing benchmarks run on the founder's personal LinkedIn.
  Rich Vitaro's pedigree (AlixPartners, Parthenon) is currently under-told on the site
  and should anchor the LinkedIn voice.
  \item \textbf{Email-first capture (Williams).} A named newsletter, a sign-up form on
  every page, and an archive. This is the single mechanism that most directly serves
  ``2,000+ decision makers every 8--12 weeks,'' because it is the only channel Creo
  would own outright.
  \item \textbf{Write once, publish everywhere (Williams).} One cornerstone piece per
  cycle becomes two or three newsletter issues, a byline (CEOWORLD-style outlets accept
  practitioner contributors), a LinkedIn series, and a podcast or roundtable topic.
  \item \textbf{Sequence launches instead of posting ad hoc (QP).} Anchor asset
  $\rightarrow$ wire release $\rightarrow$ guest podcast $\rightarrow$ weekly derivative
  posts $\rightarrow$ founder amplification. Creo ran the first step of this in 2022--23;
  the benchmark shows what the rest of the chain looks like.
  \item \textbf{A calendar link on every page (QP, Williams).} Replace ``Contact us +
  phone'' with ``book a 30-minute working session'' and a short form.
  \item \textbf{PE-native events and a PR habit (Craig).} ACG Boston, PEI Operating
  Partners Forum, and a wire release for every meaningful hire, survey, or product
  change.
  \item \textbf{Show the logos and the speed numbers (Bridgetown).} A row of
  sponsor names Creo has permission to show is worth more than another anonymized
  story. Pair it with a few hard, testable claims (``diligence read-out in 10 days'')
  of the kind Bridgetown leads with.
  \item \textbf{State the data-handling posture (Bridgetown).} If Creo markets
  AI-assisted delivery, one short paragraph on how deal data and MNPI are handled
  answers a sponsor's first objection before it is raised.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Avoid the failure modes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Bursty cadence.} Craig Group goes quiet for most of a year; QP's engine
  is seven weeks old; Creo's own hub has been dormant since November 2023. The brief's
  8--12-week rhythm is only a differentiator if it holds for four quarters.
  \item \textbf{Unaudited reach claims.} A headline ``50,000 readers'' or ``\$1.4B of
  enterprise value'' without a source is a liability under a PE due-diligence lens.
  Report list size, open rate, and follower growth as measured.
  \item \textbf{Hero dependence.} Williams and QP are one person each. Creo should put
  team authors on content, not just the founder.
  \item \textbf{Inconsistent numbers and placeholder copy.} QP's \$1.2B/\$1.4B and
  8--12-week/90--180-day conflicts are exactly what a diligence-trained buyer notices
  first. One set of numbers, reviewed before publishing.
  \item \textbf{Brand sprawl.} Four Stelling-led brands dilute QP's offer. Keep every
  Creo asset under one name and one URL.
  \item \textbf{The faceless vendor.} Bridgetown names no one, so it reads as
  software. Creo's people are the product and should be on every page.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Differentiate where none of them can}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Team over hero.} A real, named, multi-person bench with bios and
  attributed testimonials. Of the four, only Craig Group comes close.
  \item \textbf{Attributed proof.} Even two or three case studies with a named sponsor
  or portfolio CEO on record \emph{and} an outcome figure would exceed the entire
  benchmark set: Bridgetown names clients without outcomes; the others give outcomes
  without clients. This is a Discovery-meeting ask.
  \item \textbf{A recurring owned asset.} An annual Creo survey or benchmark (reviving
  the 2022 motion) and a named diagnostic as the gated lead magnet. QP's only tool
  lives on a separate site; Craig has none; Williams's is a free book chapter.
  \item \textbf{P\&L-wide scope.} Craig Group is a sales-and-marketing shop; QP
  excludes ``pure advisory''; Williams sells decision tools. Creo can own the full
  value-creation bridge --- strategy, growth, operations, human capital --- in one firm.
  \item \textbf{Boston/Northeast sponsor density.} Both PE-facing boutiques are
  Texas-centric, and Bridgetown has no regional presence at all. Creo's geography is
  an asset in a relationship-driven market.
  \item \textbf{Judgment and accountability, not research capacity.} Bridgetown
  makes the fact base fast and cheap. That raises the value of what it explicitly
  leaves to ``human judgement'': choosing the plan, owning the EBITDA bridge, and
  getting management to execute. Creo should position on that line and treat tools
  like Teamroom as leverage, not as a threat to argue against. A Creo team running
  AI-built diligence and finishing it with senior operating judgment is cheaper than
  a large firm and more accountable than software. Bridgetown already markets to
  ``small consulting firms'' as partners, so this is also a possible alliance.
\end{enumerate}
